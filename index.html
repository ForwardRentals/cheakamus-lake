Cheakamus Lake

Three and a half kilometres of old-growth cedar and hemlock, almost no climbing, and then a lake the colour of glacier milk stirred into ink. The easiest big-payoff walk on the whole corridor — and somehow still the quiet one.

Lat ≈50.02° N Lon ≈122.93° W Elev ≈840 m To lake ≈3.5 km Gain ≈100 m To Singing Creek ≈7 km
The Trail, Kilometre by Kilometre

A flat line through very tall things.

0.0km

Trailhead

End of the gravel road above the Cheakamus River. Pit toilet, information board, and the sudden quiet of big timber. Read the signage — park rules here change seasonally.

≈1.5km

The big trunks

The forest goes properly old: cedar and hemlock wide enough to hide a person behind, moss to the knees, river noise drifting up through the trees. The trail barely bothers to climb.

≈3.5km

Lake outlet

The trees open and the colour hits — glacier-fed turquoise stretching back toward the icefields. Campsites, log-jam foregrounds, and most people’s turnaround.

≈7.0km

Singing Creek

Carry on along the north shore — rolling, rooty, worth it — to a quieter beach and a second set of campsites. This is where the day-trippers aren’t.

From the Notebook

The lake that asks almost nothing.

Garibaldi Provincial Park mostly makes you earn it. Panorama Ridge is a full day. Wedgemount is a wall. And then there’s Cheakamus Lake, tucked into the park’s southwest corner behind Whistler, where the entire price of admission is a forest walk you could do in running shoes: about 3.5 kilometres one-way, with something like a hundred metres of gain — barely enough to notice.

The approach starts near Cheakamus Crossing and Function Junction at the south end of Whistler, then follows 7–8 kilometres of gravel road to the trailhead. In a normal summer an ordinary 2WD car manages it fine, though it’s a forest road, not a highway — check current conditions before you commit, especially early or late in the season.

“The trees do the first act. The colour does the second.”

What makes the walk itself worth writing down is the forest. This is genuine old growth — western redcedar and hemlock on a scale that resets your sense of what a tree is — and the trail runs level through it, so you spend the whole approach looking up instead of at your feet. Then the canopy breaks and the lake arrives all at once: big, cold, and that impossible glacial turquoise, milky with rock flour, walled in by the park’s ridgelines.

Most people stop at the outlet. If the shoreline crowd bothers you, keep going — the trail continues along the north shore to Singing Creek, about 7 kilometres from the car in total, trading a little rooty up-and-down for beaches you might have to yourself. Either way you’re back at the trailhead by dinner, which is the whole charm: an alpine-lake reward for a valley-floor effort.

Field Notes

Six things people come home talking about.

i.

The colour

Glacier-fed turquoise that photographs like a filter and isn’t one. Brightest under summer sun, moodier — and arguably better — under cloud.

ii.

The old growth

Cedar and hemlock big enough that the walk in would justify itself even if there were no lake at the end of it.

iii.

The effort-to-payoff ratio

Roughly a hundred metres of gain for a full glacial-lake reveal. Nothing else in the corridor gives you this much for this little.

iv.

The swim, briefly

Swimmable if you’re brave. The kind of cold that ends the conversation in about forty-five seconds and gets retold for years.

v.

Singing Creek’s quiet

Push past the outlet and the crowd thins to almost nothing. A gravel beach, the creek, and a lot of lake to look at.

vi.

The mosquitoes

Remembered less fondly. Early summer here is bug country — bring repellent or donate blood. They ease off as the season dries out.

When to Go

The lake keeps its own calendar.

It sits around 800-and-some metres, so the season runs longer than the alpine — but this is still Garibaldi. Snow lingers, bugs bloom, and access rules shift with the seasons.

May – June
Snow usually clears earlier here than the high country, but the road and trail can be soft and the river runs loud. Peak mosquito season arrives as things warm — come armed. Check road and trail conditions before driving up.
Quiet, buggy
July – August
Prime time. The turquoise is at full saturation, the gravel road is normally fine for 2WD, and the outlet gets its daily crowd. Go early, or go past it to Singing Creek. Bugs taper as summer dries out.
Peak colour
September – October
The sleeper pick. Cooler air, thinner crowds, low-angle light on the water. The colour softens as glacial melt slows, but the forest more than covers the difference.
Locals’ season
November – April
Snow country. The road is generally not a summer-car proposition and the trail becomes a winter route for people with the gear and judgment for it. Not the version of this trip this guide describes.
Experts only

Garibaldi Provincial Park has day-use and overnight rules that change seasonally. Whatever you’ve heard from a friend’s trip last year may be out of date — check the BC Parks page for the park before you go.

Staying the Night

Two camps, one easy carry.

Because the walk in is so gentle, Cheakamus is one of the friendliest overnight introductions in Garibaldi — a real backcountry camp that doesn’t demand a real backcountry approach.

≈3.5 km in

Cheakamus Lake camp

Sites near the outlet where the trail first meets the water. The short carry makes it popular — expect neighbours on summer weekends, and book ahead.

≈7 km in

Singing Creek camp

Another 3.5 km along the north shore buys you the quieter beach. The extra distance filters the crowd better than any reservation system could.

Before you pack Backcountry camping here is managed through BC Parks reservations, and Garibaldi’s overnight rules shift seasonally — confirm the current system before you commit to a date. And this is black bear country: store food properly, cook away from your tent, and pack out everything.
Honest Note

What the postcards leave out.

The water is beautiful and it is cold — glacier-fed, all season, no exceptions. Swim if you like, but treat it as a plunge, not a pool, and keep an eye on anyone small.

Early summer belongs to the mosquitoes. This is the single most common complaint about an otherwise gentle trip, and it’s a fair one — June at the lake without repellent is a character-building exercise.

Black bears are common through this valley. That’s not a reason to stay home; it’s a reason to keep food sealed and stored properly, make a bit of noise on quiet stretches of trail, and never leave a pack unattended on the shore.

And the gravel road: normally fine for a regular car in summer, but “normally” is doing some work in that sentence. Washboard, potholes, and early-season surprises happen. Check conditions, drive slowly, and leave nothing visible in the car at the trailhead.

Trailhead Support

This one’s a hike, not a paddle.

No rental fleet waits at this trailhead, and that’s part of the appeal. But if you want help with the logistics — overnight gear dropped to you in the corridor, or a local guide to run the day so you just walk — the same Squamish outfit behind these guides handles both.

Gear & guide bookings

Missing the water already?

If the turquoise leaves you wanting an actual day on a lake, the corridor’s canoe rentals live down in Squamish — Squamish Canoe Rental delivers boats (up to three paddlers each) to the paddling lakes at the other end of the highway, or you can pick up and drop off at their Squamish base yourself.

Questions & Answers

What people actually ask about Cheakamus Lake.

How hard is the Cheakamus Lake hike?

Genuinely easy by Garibaldi standards. It’s about 3.5 km one-way to the lake outlet with only around 100 m of elevation gain — a mostly level forest walk on good trail. Most reasonably mobile people, including kids, can manage it comfortably. Continuing along the north shore to Singing Creek makes it roughly 7 km one-way with some rolling, rooty sections, but still nothing technical in summer conditions.

Can a regular 2WD car get to the trailhead?

Normally, yes. From the Cheakamus Crossing / Function Junction area at the south end of Whistler, it’s about 7–8 km of gravel forest road to the trailhead, and in a typical summer an ordinary car handles it at a careful pace. That said, it’s a gravel road — conditions vary with weather and season, so check current reports before you go, especially in spring or fall.

Do I need a day pass or permit for Cheakamus Lake?

Garibaldi Provincial Park has day-use and overnight rules that change seasonally, and they’ve changed more than once in recent years — so rather than repeat something that may be stale, we’ll say this plainly: check the BC Parks page for Garibaldi before every trip. It takes two minutes and saves a wasted drive.

Can you camp at Cheakamus Lake?

Yes — there are backcountry campsites near the lake outlet (about 3.5 km in) and at Singing Creek (about 7 km in), reserved through BC Parks. The gentle approach makes this one of the best first backcountry overnights in the corridor. Singing Creek is the quieter of the two; the extra 3.5 km of shoreline trail does the crowd-filtering for you.

Can you swim in Cheakamus Lake?

If you’re brave. The lake is glacier-fed and stays seriously cold all summer — think short, loud plunge rather than afternoon float. On a hot August day it’s a glorious shock. There are no lifeguards and the water is deep and cold enough to take seriously, so keep swims short and stay close to shore.

Are there bears? What about bugs?

Both, honestly. Black bears are common in this valley — store food properly (especially overnight), keep a clean camp, and make some noise on quiet stretches. Mosquitoes are the other resident: early summer can be intense at the lake, so bring repellent and maybe a head net for June trips. Both problems fade as the season goes on.

Is Cheakamus Lake a good alternative to Joffre Lakes?

It’s the classic Whistler-side answer when Joffre passes are gone or the parking lot news scares you off. You trade Joffre’s stacked-lakes drama for a bigger single lake, far older forest, and a much easier walk — and generally a calmer day. Same turquoise family, quieter cousin.

Can you canoe or paddle on Cheakamus Lake?

There’s no boat launch and no rentals at the lake — anything you paddle, you carry 3.5 km through the forest first, which is why almost nobody does. If a paddling day is what you’re really after, the drive-up canoe lakes around Squamish down the highway are the practical answer, or browse the other Sea to Sky Trails guides for lakes where the boat meets you at the shore.
